# What is Cascade

Cascade is HP's privacy-first data and segmentation engine that unifies customer data from many systems, enforces consent, and generates audience segments for personalized communications. It does not send messages—delivery happens through systems like SFMC, Message Hub, and HP apps. Cascade ensures full data governance, powers multi-channel personalization, and has maintained zero privacy incidents since launch

What Cascade Is: Cascade is an HP-built data platform (launched in early 2020) for privacy-centric, multi-channel customer communications and analytics. It was created in response to a major 2019 privacy incident in Europe that led to fines and marketing restrictions, driving HP to build a system with strict privacy compliance from the ground up. Cascade serves as a unified hub where all customer engagement data is collected and processed, and where marketing campaigns and customer journeys are centrally defined and analyzed. By design, Cascade owns and processes all customer data in-house, ensuring HP maintains full control over data and reduces dependency on external vendors. The primary business goal of Cascade is to increase customer engagement and revenue through smarter, more personalized communication across HP's product and service lines.

Scope and Boundaries: Cascade's scope encompasses end-to-end data handling for customer outreach – it ingests raw data from various source systems (e.g. sales, support, device telemetry), builds cleaned and enriched data products, enforces customer privacy/consent rules, and produces targetable audience segments used in campaigns. Cascade then exposes these audience segments to marketing channels like email, mobile apps, and advertising platforms for execution of campaigns. In essence, Cascade is the back-end data and analytics engine for HP's customer communications.

However, Cascade does not itself execute outbound communications or generate marketing content. It does not send emails or push notifications directly – those actions are handled by downstream "touchpoint" systems such as Salesforce Marketing Cloud (for emails), HP's Message Hub (for mobile app push notifications), and similar delivery platforms. Likewise, Cascade isn't a campaign UI or journey planner; the front-end for campaign design and orchestration is HP's Engagement Studio, which marketers use to define campaign rules and schedules. Engagement Studio determines when and how communications happen (the orchestration control plane), whereas Cascade provides the data and segments to fulfill those rules (the data/analytics plane). Cascade also doesn't handle content creation – actual message content is managed in content repositories (e.g. Content Stack) outside Cascade. In summary, Cascade focuses on data and audience selection, leaving customer-facing message delivery and journey control to other systems.

Business Value: By centralizing customer data and segmentation, Cascade enables HP to understand customers better and communicate intelligently across multiple channels. All campaigns, whether email offers, in-app alerts, or push notifications, draw from the same data truths and consent checks in Cascade, leading to consistent, personalized experiences. Importantly, Cascade's privacy-first design ensures that every outreach is compliant with the customer's consent choices and privacy laws – a non-negotiable in the wake of past violations. Since Cascade's launch, HP has had zero privacy incidents in marketing data usage, indicating the effectiveness of Cascade's safeguards. Additionally, owning the data pipeline gives HP agility in creating new campaigns and deriving insights (rather than relying on vendor black-box tools), and it provides a unified view of campaign performance across all business units.

System Boundaries – What Cascade Does vs. Does Not Do:

Cascade does:

- Ingest and unify data from various internal and external sources (e.g. support databases, online store transactions, device telemetry feeds) into HP's analytics cloud (Data OS).
- Transform and store data in structured tables, applying business logic and cleaning (e.g. decrypting files, combining and flattening records) to create a single source of truth for customer data ready for marketing use.
- Enforce privacy & consent rules on all data used for marketing: Cascade checks customer consent preferences (marketing opt-in/opt-out, channel permissions, etc.) and filters out anyone who hasn't consented before including them in a campaign segment. It uses a "Cascade ID" to link all of a customer's identifiers, ensuring that a global opt-out truly excludes the customer across all datasets and channels.
- Build audience segments for campaigns, combining various data criteria (e.g. recent purchases, device type, region, subscription status) to select the right customers for a given marketing intent. These segments are typically lists of customer identifiers who qualify for a campaign.
- Provide segments and insights to downstream systems: Cascade makes the results of its data processing available via APIs or data exports. For example, it supplies segment files to an "EOS API" (Cascade's API service) which Engagement Studio calls to fetch campaign audiences. It drops audience files to S3 or SFTP for systems like Salesforce Marketing Cloud (for email campaigns) to pick up. It also exposes cleaned data for analysts via an internal data catalog and dashboards (so teams can run reports on campaign performance, etc.).
- Log and monitor campaign data and privacy actions: Cascade keeps audit logs of any data deletions (for DSR requests) and maintains records of who was sent what campaign, providing traceability and compliance evidence.

Cascade does NOT:

- Send communications directly – it doesn't send emails or push notifications itself. Instead, it hands off segments or triggers to execution systems (e.g. HP Message Hub sends the push notifications, Salesforce Marketing Cloud sends emails). Cascade ensures the right people are targeted; the actual sending is done elsewhere.
- Decide campaign timing or journey logic – scheduling and sequencing of customer touches (e.g. send email then app notification if no response) is orchestrated by Engagement Studio, not Cascade. Cascade provides data (e.g. segment lists, customer attributes) to Engagement Studio's rules engine, but it doesn't contain campaign business rules itself.
- Create marketing content or creatives – Cascade deals with data (who to contact, when), not the message content. Content (like email templates, images, text) is managed in content management systems (e.g. Content Stack) and selected by Engagement Studio or channel systems. Cascade simply ensures the data (like customer name, product details) is available to merge into those templates.
- Replace CRM or subscription systems – Cascade consumes customer data and outputs segments, but it isn't a CRM tool on its own. For instance, customer subscriptions are managed in other systems; Cascade will use that info (say, only target non-subscribers for a promo) but not manage the subscription itself. Similarly, Cascade checks consent status but the source of truth for consent (e.g. a central consent database or service) feeds into Cascade; Cascade doesn't provide the UI for customers to opt-in/out.
- Provide a user-facing interface (beyond internal tools) – Users of Cascade are mostly internal (data engineers, analysts, architects). Marketers interface with Cascade only indirectly via Engagement Studio or possibly via an EOS Portal (a web UI for engineers to manage Cascade's data dictionary and debug segments). Cascade doesn't have a marketing-friendly GUI for campaign design – that's Engagement Studio's role. The EOS Portal is more of an admin tool to view what data/fields exist and to troubleshoot segment definitions in Cascade.

By clearly defining these boundaries, Cascade acts as the analytics powerhouse and data steward behind HP's customer communications, in partnership with front-end campaign tools and delivery channels.

2. Architecture Decomposition

High-Level Architecture: Cascade's architecture can be viewed in four layers: Data Sources, the Cascade Processing Platform, the Cascade API & Data Services, and Touchpoint Systems. At a high level, data flows into Cascade from source systems, is processed within Cascade's data platform, and flows out to customer-facing channels via APIs or data feeds, with privacy enforcement at its core. The schematic below illustrates this end-to-end flow:

Major Components & Services:

Data Sources (Upstream Systems): These are the external systems that produce raw data consumed by Cascade. They include internal HP systems and some external platforms: for example,

- Support Warranty system (Azure-based, provides warranty & Care Pack info),
- HP Online Store and E-commerce platforms (via Adobe's data pipeline, providing purchase transaction data),
- WPP (Web Print Platform) databases (printer registration and usage data),
- Mastiff (an internal campaign tracking system sending data via SFTP),
- Branch.io (third-party service for mobile app link analytics),
- HP In-App Messaging systems (for HP Smart and HPX apps), and various telemetry and profile services. Each source has an established ingestion method (discussed in Section 3) – e.g. daily file drops, API calls, database queries. These sources feed customer attributes like purchase history, support status, device events, etc., into Cascade.

Cascade Data Platform (Processing & Storage): The heart of Cascade is a data processing platform built on HP's Data OS (an AWS-based analytics environment leveraging Databricks/Spark and S3 data lake storage). Cascade's code (primarily PySpark jobs and notebooks) runs on Databricks clusters, which perform the heavy lifting of data ingestion, transformation, and aggregation. Raw data lands in what's conceptually the "Engineering Room" (Cascade's raw zone) – here data is stored in raw form and initial cleaning happens. Next, refined datasets are produced and moved to the "Operations Room" – this is Cascade's core production data store containing final, ready-to-use tables. The Operations Room holds key derived data products such as segment tables, profile tables, and reference data. Importantly, data in the Operations Room is not pre-filtered by consent – it's a complete set of marketing data, but segmentation queries must apply consent rules when reading it. Cascade uses AWS S3 storage with Delta Lake tables (via Databricks/Unity Catalog) to store these datasets, enabling large-scale data processing and time-travel (for versioning) capabilities. Some Cascade data also resides in Redshift (an AWS data warehouse) – historically, certain tables were loaded to Redshift to serve analytics or external access needs. In total, Cascade manages on the order of hundreds of data tables (400+ tables are under Cascade's ownership) spanning these storage systems.

Key Data Stores within Cascade:

- Raw Landing Tables (Engineering Room): e.g. raw support cases, raw e-commerce transactions, raw telemetry events – typically one per source, stored as ingested.
- Profile Tables: unified entities that link identifiers (Customer profile, Device profile, Subscription profile, etc.). Each profile table has a Cascade ID key, which is a unique internal identifier that ties together all of a customer's data across sources. This linking is crucial for tasks like DSR deletion (to find all data for one person) and for multi-source segmentation (to ensure, e.g., matching a customer's printer data to their HP account).
- Consent Records: tables that track the latest consent status for each identifier (e.g. a table of printer device IDs with their marketing consent status, another for app consent, etc.). The "Consent Current State" system maintains these tables so that Cascade always knows whether a given device or user is allowed to be contacted.
- Operational Reference Tables: small, static tables used universally (countries list, product catalog mapping, etc.) stored in the Operations Room for reference. These are generally not subject to consent (they contain no personal data).
- Segment & Analytics Tables: results of Cascade's processing that feed campaigns or reports. For example, a "segment membership" table might list which customers qualified for each campaign segment, or an "engagement metrics" table in the Analytics Room aggregating campaign results for reporting. The Analytics Room is a separate area where Cascade outputs data specifically structured for dashboards and insights (not used for sending campaigns, but for analysis).

Cascade Services (API and Supporting Tools): On top of the data platform, Cascade provides services that allow other systems (and users) to interact with Cascade data in a controlled way:

- EOS API (Cascade Segmentation API): This is a RESTful API that Engagement Studio and other consumers call to retrieve segments or to query what data is available. When Engagement Studio needs to execute a campaign, it may call EOS API asking "give me all customers matching campaign X's criteria." The EOS API then runs a segmentation job against Cascade's data, applies all relevant consent filters in real-time (consulting the data dictionary and consent tables), and returns the eligible customers. This API resides as part of Cascade's production system and is essentially the control plane for campaign execution – it orchestrates the final step of reading the data and delivering results, ensuring things like do-not-contact rules are enforced at query time. The EOS API also updates some status (for example, it might log that a campaign was pulled or mark segment generation complete). Technically, this API could be implemented as jobs on Databricks or as microservices hitting the Delta tables; either way, it's a defined interface for retrieving Cascade outputs.
- Data Dictionary and EOS Portal: To manage the complexity of many data sources and consent rules, Cascade includes a Data Dictionary – a metadata repository listing all data fields and tables and indicating what consent category each field falls under. The EOS Portal is a web interface used by engineers to view this dictionary and to debug how data flows are working. For example, if someone needs to know "does Cascade have field X for customers, and if so under what consent?", they can look it up in the data dictionary (via the portal). The portal also supports operations like viewing loaded campaign rulesets and monitoring segment creation jobs for debugging. This is an operational tool that sits alongside Cascade – not used by end-users or marketers, but crucial for the Cascade team to manage the system.
- Consent Management Integration: While not a separate "service" with an API, Cascade's architecture explicitly integrates with HP's consent and privacy systems. For instance, there is a pipeline that regularly ingests Data Subject Requests (DSRs) (requests from customers to delete or not use their data) from HP's central privacy office into Cascade. Cascade's processing then automatically executes those requests across its datasets (using the Cascade ID to find all related records and purging them). The design ensures an incoming DSR propagates to Cascade's data within 24 hours via this automated job. The results (deletion logs, suppression flags) are then available for compliance auditing. This integration can be thought of as Cascade's privacy enforcement service – it's tightly coupled with the data pipeline rather than a user-facing API, but it is a key architectural component fulfilling one of Cascade's core principles (Privacy First).

Touchpoint & Downstream Systems: These are the systems that consume Cascade's outputs to actually contact customers or further process the data. Major ones include:

- Engagement Studio – HP's campaign management tool (internal application) that triggers campaigns and interacts with Cascade via the EOS API. When a campaign is about to launch, Engagement Studio uses Cascade to get the audience. Engagement Studio also controls sequencing (ensuring customers get messages in the intended order and timing) and handles channel logic (deciding whether to send an email vs. an app notification based on campaign design), using Cascade's data as input.
- HP Consumer Apps and Push Notification System – e.g. the HP Smart App and HPX App (HP's mobile applications) and the Message Hub service that sends push notifications to those apps. Cascade's segments intended for in-app messaging are delivered through a pipeline that involves writing the segment data to an S3 location and then calling the P2 API (also known as P2 "ingestion" API) to ingest that data into a DynamoDB store used by the apps. Specifically, for any new segment, Cascade will output a file of user IDs to S3; an event (or scheduled job) triggers the P2 API to load this file into DynamoDB (the P2 system's data store). Once in Dynamo, the P2 Client API (used by the HP apps) can query what campaigns a given user is eligible for, and the app will then display the appropriate in-app message. Additionally, when segments are updated, Cascade or P2 may send callbacks/notifications to other systems (for example, informing MessageHub that a new push segment is ready). This integration is how mobile app and push channels get Cascade's data.
- Salesforce Marketing Cloud (SFMC) – the platform used for email campaigns. Cascade's integration here is via batch file exchange. When Cascade identifies an email target segment (say, users to receive a promotion email), it generates an output file (typically CSV, often encrypted for security) and drops it on an SFTP server that SFMC monitors. SFMC then automatically picks up the file and runs an email send automation for those contacts. This is done on a schedule (e.g. daily or campaign-triggered). Cascade monitors these drops – if a file fails to appear or is empty, that can indicate either "no new targets" or an upstream issue (see Ops section on monitoring). This mechanism is how email channel gets fed by Cascade.
- Advertising Platforms – Cascade can also feed external marketing channels like Facebook and Google for retargeting campaigns. In such cases, segments from Cascade might be exported in a format to upload to these ad platforms (e.g. a list of hashed emails for a Facebook Custom Audience). The Cascade slide deck mentions these as consumers, though the exact integration likely involves either manual upload or an automated export pipeline. These ensure paid media campaigns are using Cascade's carefully filtered segments as well.
- Analytics & BI Tools – Finally, internal analytics systems (BI dashboards, data science notebooks, etc.) consume Cascade's Analytics Room data. For example, a Power BI or Tableau dashboard might connect to a Cascade analytics table (possibly via Redshift or via Databricks cluster) to display campaign performance metrics to business stakeholders. This is facilitated by the Cascade team giving access to those tables (with permission controls). It doesn't involve real-time APIs but rather read access to data. The benefit is that everyone – from marketing analysts to data scientists – can get a consistent view of customer and campaign data from Cascade's outputs.

Data Flow & Data Plane vs Control Plane: Cascade's architecture cleanly separates the data plane (the pipeline that moves and transforms data) from the control plane (the logic that orchestrates when/how data is used for communications). In practice:

- The Data Plane is the sequence of batch and streaming jobs that ingest raw data into Cascade's storage, apply transformations, and maintain up-to-date tables (it covers everything up to having the data ready in Operations/Analytics rooms). This plane is always on, continually updating Cascade's datasets in the background (mostly via scheduled jobs). It is also responsible for applying any data privacy actions like DSR deletions within the data storage itself. The data plane ensures that at any given time, Cascade's data repository reflects the latest state of customers (minus those who opted out or were deleted).
- The Control Plane in Cascade consists of the triggers and query-time filters that actually drive campaign execution. Engagement Studio and other consumers operate on this control plane: e.g., a marketer triggers a campaign (control signal), Engagement Studio calls Cascade's API to fetch a segment (control-plane operation), and Cascade's API in turn orchestrates reading the data (data-plane access) with consent filters, then returns results. The decision of when to call, what rules to apply (like "this is a marketing campaign, so apply marketing consent filters"), and where to send results are all control plane aspects. Cascade's design pushes certain logic to this layer – notably consent enforcement is handled in the control plane (API) rather than by pre-splitting the data in storage. This means the Operations Room can hold all data (maximizing flexibility and data reuse), and the API will dynamically exclude any disallowed records when assembling a segment. This separation adds flexibility: if consent policies change or new rules are needed, they can be updated in the API layer without re-engineering the entire data pipeline.

Another example of control vs data plane separation is how Engagement Studio orchestrates journeys (control) while Cascade simply provides the data feed (data). The benefit is that Cascade's data plane can run continuously (ensuring fresh data), independent of how or when campaigns are executed. And when the control plane calls upon it, the data plane serves data with all policies applied. This architecture also means if Cascade's data plane is updated or improved (say, new data sources added), the control plane consumers automatically benefit from richer data without needing changes – and vice versa, new campaign strategies can be implemented by control systems leveraging the existing data plane.

Component Integration & Interactions: Putting it together, a typical interaction might be:

- A daily job in Cascade ingests new Online Store transactions (Data Source) into a Delta table in the Engineering Room, processes it (Cascade Platform), and updates a Purchases table in the Operations Room.
- Engagement Studio has a campaign rule like "If customer bought X but not subscribed to Y, send an offer in the app". It triggers that campaign for today.
- Engagement Studio calls the EOS API (Cascade Service) with the rule parameters. Cascade's API knows from the data dictionary which data it needs (e.g. Purchases table, Subscription table, plus consent flags) and what consent checks to apply (marketing consent since it's an offer, plus channel consent for app).
- Cascade (via the API) queries the Operations Room data (Cascade Platform), joining purchase and subscription tables via Cascade ID, filtering out anyone without proper consent (Consent tables consulted).
- The API produces a segment list of qualifying HPIDs (customer IDs) and returns it to Engagement Studio.
- Engagement Studio sends those IDs to the HPX App via Message Hub (Touchpoint system) to display the in-app offer. The app, using P2 Client API, might double-check the user's segment membership from DynamoDB (which was updated via the P2 ingestion when Cascade's job prior loaded segments).
- Separately, overnight, Cascade's DSR process runs and sees that one of the customers in that campaign yesterday filed a deletion request. Cascade's profile tables link that customer's HPID, and the process deletes their records from Purchases, Subscription, etc., and also adds their email to a suppression list. Tomorrow, even if that customer would qualify for some segment, Cascade's API will exclude them (due to no data found or them being marked opted-out).

Through these interactions, Cascade ensures data from many sources converges in one place, gets processed with uniform rules, and is then disseminated out to all the right channels. The architecture is designed for scalability (Spark jobs on large data volumes), flexibility (schema changes or new data sources can be incorporated in the pipeline), and compliance (centralized enforcement of privacy and consent at the point of use). There is also built-in redundancy in the sense that core tables are persisted and can be reprocessed if needed (you can re-run a segment query, or backfill data if a job was missed, since raw data is stored).

Control vs Data Example – Clean Rooms: In Cascade's early architecture, the team created separate physical datasets for every combination of consent (often called "clean rooms"). For example, a "Printer Marketing room" had only printer data for users who gave marketing consent, a "Printer Analytics room" for analytics consent, etc.. This was extremely safe (data that wasn't allowed for a purpose simply didn't exist in that dataset), but it meant massive duplication of data into many tables and complicated pipelines. In the current architecture, they eliminated these duplicate rooms: now there is broadly one Operations Room where data is stored once, and clean rooms are a logical concept – effectively, a clean room = (data + consent filter) rather than a separate copy. The dynamic filtering at the API level serves the purpose of the old clean rooms without needing multiple datasets. This change was a key architectural evolution that simplified Cascade (see Section 5).

Security and Access Architecture: Cascade operates within HP's secure cloud environment, and there are mechanisms in place for cross-system and cross-team access:

- For any data source that lies in a different cloud or account, secure transfer methods are used. e.g., the Support Org data in Azure uses MuleSoft (an enterprise transfer service) to securely copy files from Azure to AWS for Cascade. For internal AWS account boundaries, Cascade does not use static credentials; instead it uses AWS IAM AssumeRole policies to allow Cascade's Databricks jobs to read from/write to other teams' S3 buckets as needed. Each team grants Cascade a role with least-privilege access to the required data, and Cascade's cluster assumes that role at runtime to pull the data. This avoids sharing long-lived AWS keys and keeps access auditable.
- Data at rest in Cascade's S3 buckets is typically stored in encrypted form (AWS S3 default encryption). Sensitive outputs like files for SFMC are additionally PGP-encrypted before transfer. Secrets (passwords, API keys) needed for jobs (e.g., an SFTP password or a database credential) are kept in AWS Secrets Manager, not in code or configs. Cascade's Terraform scripts create placeholder secret entries, and then secret values are manually entered in AWS by authorized leads, ensuring they never appear in GitHub. The Databricks jobs retrieve these at runtime.
- Access control to Cascade's data for internal users is managed via a combination of Unity Catalog (for Databricks tables) and a custom JIRA-based Access Tagging system. Each Cascade table has an associated "access tag" – essentially a label that can be granted to users who request access. For example, a data analyst from another team might request access to "Cascade's printer engagement table" by selecting the corresponding access tag in a JIRA form; upon approval, they get read access to that table. This is a fine-grained access model (Cascade chose one-tag-per-table for high control). However, for automated jobs and services, Cascade uses Terraform-managed IAM groups in Databricks (Unity Catalog groups) to grant permissions, since the JIRA system doesn't support service accounts. In practice, human users rarely need direct access to raw Cascade data (except for exploratory or debug purposes), and production workflows use service identities that are pre-approved.

In summary, Cascade's architecture is composed of robust data pipelines on a cloud data lake, an API-centric consumption layer for segments, and tight integrations with both upstream data sources and downstream engagement channels. The design emphasizes privacy, centralization, and control, aligning with the founding principles:

- Privacy by design – privacy considerations are baked in at every layer (from segregated data handling to dynamic consent checks).
- Centralized data & analytics – all campaign-related data flows through Cascade to ensure one source of truth for customer journeys.
- HP's ownership & control – the architecture avoids external black boxes; HP controls data storage, processing logic, and integration points to retain maximum flexibility and oversight.

3. Core Pipelines & Workflows

Cascade comprises numerous data pipelines and workflows. This section describes the key pipelines from data ingestion through transformation to serving results, how they are triggered/orchestrated, and how Cascade handles failures, retries, and critical dependencies. We will also highlight any specific SLAs (service level agreements) or timing constraints that Cascade pipelines adhere to, and identify the critical paths where a failure has major impact.

Key Data Ingestion & Processing Pipelines (Ingest → Transform → Serve)

Most of Cascade's data flows can be categorized into a set of standard pipeline patterns that correspond to the source systems. There are eight main data ingestion pipelines, each handling a particular data source or use-case. Despite differences in source and details, they follow a similar high-level shape:

- Retrieve new data from source – via scheduled job (daily or periodic) or event trigger.
- Land raw data in Cascade (Engineering Room) – often dumping into an S3 landing zone or a raw Delta table.
- Transform and integrate – decrypt, decompress or flatten as needed; join with reference data; and append to a cumulative table or generate a new derived table.
- Load into final tables (Operations/Analytics Room) – make the processed data available for use in segments or analysis.
- (Optional) Trigger downstream action – if this data directly feeds a campaign or system, signal that new data is ready.

In addition to these source-ingestion pipelines, Cascade has internal pipelines such as:

- Consent State Pipeline: updates the "Consent Current State" tables for each app/device daily, taking the latest consent logs from source systems (like user preference center or product telemetries) and ensuring Cascade's consent reference is up-to-date. This pipeline ensures any changes in consent (opt-in or opt-out) are reflected before the next campaign run.
- DSR Execution Workflow: as described, runs daily to process any new deletion/suppression requests – it joins across all profile tables by Cascade ID, deletes data in all relevant tables, and logs the actions. This is effectively a daily batch job that might run at off-peak hours (since it can be heavy).
- Segmentation Jobs: These are not time-scheduled but on-demand pipelines triggered by API calls. When Engagement Studio triggers a campaign, Cascade may spin up a Spark job to generate the segment (if it's a complex query). This job reads from all the above pipeline outputs (the Operations Room data) to compute the final list. In architecture terms, this is a pipeline too – it just happens in real-time. It's optimized for speed (likely using pre-indexed data or pushing filters down) so that even large campaigns can be computed quickly (ideally in seconds or a few minutes).

Triggering Mechanisms & Orchestration

Cascade's workflows use a mix of scheduled triggers and event-driven triggers:

- Scheduled Batches: Most ingestion jobs (the first 7 patterns above) are triggered on a daily schedule (e.g., every night at 2 AM UTC or similar). The timing is set such that source systems have dropped their files or made data available by then. For example, the Support Org Azure transfer might run at 1 AM, so Cascade's job to pick up and process it runs at 2 AM. These schedules are configured in Databricks Workflows or via an orchestrator (possibly Apache Airflow or AWS Data Pipeline, given mention of "retriggering Amazon Data Pipelines" in one context). The cascade team maintains a schedule of all daily jobs to avoid conflicts and manage load on the cluster. Typically the ingestion jobs are spread out during the night/morning hours. Once per day suffices for these because their source data is updated daily. They all target to finish before the business day or before any campaigns would run (so that fresh data is ready for use).
- Streaming/Near-real-time: The PSUV pipeline is event-driven (15-minute window). It likely runs as a continuously scheduled job or micro-batch stream on Databricks. Essentially, it doesn't need external triggers beyond the presence of new events in the stream; it continually checks for new registration events in the firehose and processes them. This ensures new registrations get into Cascade's segment quickly. Another near real-time aspect is the EOS API usage: when Engagement Studio calls for a segment, that triggers an immediate Spark job to compute it (if not cached). So in effect, the campaign launch is an event that triggers data processing.
- On-Demand (Human-triggered): There are a few workflows initiated manually by people:
  - Sample Size Estimation: In situations where a marketing manager wants to validate the audience size for a complicated campaign rule, they might request Cascade team to run a one-off query. This is currently a semi-manual process: an engineer uses Cascade's data to compute the segment size and returns the number. (This process is earmarked for automation in the future.)
  - Backfills & Re-runs: If a daily job fails or a source was late, an engineer can manually trigger a backfill job. For instance, if the Adobe store file didn't arrive today due to an upstream issue, once it arrives, the team can run the ingest job for that date out-of-band. They might also re-run a pipeline to recover from an error (e.g., re-run yesterday's support org job if it crashed).
  - Maintenance tasks: such as running a one-time script to reconcile data, or a GDPR audit job, etc., could be triggered as needed.

Orchestration wise, Cascade does not rely on a single monolithic scheduler for all pipelines. Instead, each pipeline can run independently, which improves fault isolation. They do ensure sequencing where necessary: e.g., if Pipeline B depends on Pipeline A's data, they schedule B to run after A or have B check that A's output exists. However, many pipelines are independent (different data sources unrelated).

One orchestrator mentioned is Amazon Data Pipeline, which may be used for certain file transfers or triggers (the mention in a monitoring video suggests an AWS pipeline was retriggered when files stopped transferring). This implies some ingestion steps might be set up as AWS Data Pipeline or Step Functions, particularly around moving data into S3 from external sources.

Engagement Studio orchestration: On the campaign side, Engagement Studio orchestrates the sequence of campaigns and calls Cascade's API at the appropriate times. For automated recurring campaigns, Engagement Studio itself is likely scheduled (e.g., "send weekly newsletter every Monday" – Engagement Studio would, on Monday, call Cascade for "newsletter audience" and then call SFMC to send emails). So those are external schedules hitting Cascade's on-demand segmentation.

Inter-pipeline Dependencies & Critical Path: Cascade's pipelines generally feed into the central repository, and campaigns draw from that. A few important dependencies and ordering:

- Many source ingestions feed data that ultimately impacts the same campaign segments. For example, a cross-sell campaign might use data from the store (who bought printer) and data from WPP (who registered printer) and perhaps consent data. For Cascade to provide an accurate segment, all relevant upstream data should be up-to-date. Therefore, Cascade's critical path each day is to complete all daily source ingestions before major campaigns execute. If one pipeline is late (say Store Transactions), segments that rely on it might miss data. In practice, the team schedules campaign execution (via Engagement Studio) after a certain time by which all ingestions are normally done. If a pipeline fails or is late beyond that time, the Cascade team either communicates to stakeholders to delay the campaign or runs an emergency backfill.
- The DSR deletion pipeline has a privacy compliance SLA – typically privacy requests must be fulfilled within 24 hours or at least before any new campaign could include that user. So the DSR job is critical to run daily without fail. If it fails on a given day, it could risk a compliance breach (customer data not deleted in time). Thus, it's closely monitored and on the critical path for compliance.
- The PSUV pipeline (new PC registration) is on a near-real-time critical path for customer experience. If it fails, new customers wouldn't get their welcome message promptly, undermining early engagement. The pipeline is designed to bypass heavy processing for speed, but that also means it's somewhat standalone. A risk is if the PSUV job stops (due to a broken stream or error), it might not be immediately obvious. They have added monitoring to ensure these streaming jobs are alive. The 15-minute SLA is an internal target for that pipeline.
- Segment Generation: Once data is in place, generating the segment for a campaign is critical to do quickly. The Cascade API or segmentation job must finish in a reasonable time (preferably under an hour for even the largest campaign, usually much faster). If segmentation took too long, it could delay or stagger multi-channel orchestration. The team likely has optimized common segmentation queries and possibly pre-materialized some data (like frequent segment building blocks) to meet these performance needs. For example, they might precompute commonly used sub-segments (like "all active HP Smart users") daily so that any campaign can quickly intersect with that rather than scan billions of records. This isn't explicitly stated, but it's a common practice in such systems to guarantee SLAs.

Failure Modes, Retries, and Recovery

Given the complexity of Cascade's pipelines, failures can and do happen, ranging from missing data to job errors. Cascade's operations have defined approaches for each scenario:

- Upstream Data Missing or Delayed: One common failure mode is that an expected input file or data set doesn't arrive on time. For instance, if the Branch API is down or the daily store transactions file wasn't delivered to S3 by ECDP, Cascade's scheduled job will find nothing new to process and may either fail or produce an empty update. Cascade's approach: The team closely monitors daily source arrivals. If a source file is missing, they might receive an alert or notice a down job. Often the pipeline is configured to tolerate a no-show (e.g., if no file, perhaps just log a warning and not crash – as seen in an SFMC file monitor: empty files are considered valid and not actually failures). If truly missing, the Cascade team will contact the source team (for internal sources) or investigate upstream logs. They can also re-run the ingestion later when the data becomes available. For example, in one incident the SFMC import files stopped appearing due to an upstream pipeline issue; once that was fixed, Cascade re-triggered the AWS Data Pipeline to push the files and ensure processing resumed. The team often has manual backfill procedures: e.g., run the job for yesterday's date again to catch the data. These backfills might be done through special notebook runs or by adjusting the job parameters and rerunning.
- Job Errors or Crashes: If a Spark job fails (due to data schema change, code bug, out-of-memory, etc.), it will typically be caught by monitoring. Cascade's approach: They have an on-call engineer alerted for job failures (see Ops section for monitoring). On failure, the team will examine logs (often via Databricks or Splunk) to identify the cause. There might be an attempt to automatically retry certain transient errors – e.g., if a cluster was temporarily unavailable or a network glitch occurred, the orchestrator might have a retry policy to run the job again after a few minutes. For more systemic errors (like a schema change), engineers will patch the code or adjust the schema and then re-run the pipeline. Because Cascade retains historical data, re-running a job is usually possible without data loss (they might overwrite or merge as needed). In critical cases (like DSR job failure), they would prioritize fixing and re-running it the same day.
- Partial Processing / Empty Outputs: Sometimes a pipeline runs successfully but produces no data (which could be correct – e.g., "no new records today" – or incorrect – e.g., the job silently skipped data). For example, an empty file scenario: The SFMC monitoring video describes that the automation job would flag a "failure" if an imported file was empty, even though an empty file might simply mean "no new subscribers to send today" which is not an error. Cascade's approach: They improved monitoring to recognize this nuance – an empty file is logged but not treated as a critical failure if empties are expected occasionally. Operators know to check: if the file is empty but should it have had data? They confirm via upstream (e.g., maybe indeed no new records). In one case, certain daily files (Medallia survey data) were often empty and that was normal. Cascade's documentation now notes that empty file ≠ error for those feeds, preventing false alarms. When empties occur, no action needed if expected. If unexpected, they treat it like missing data and investigate the source.
- Downstream Failures: If a downstream system (like SFMC or P2) fails to process Cascade's output (e.g., SFMC automation fails), the Cascade team might be alerted by downstream teams or see it via monitoring of the SFTP folder (file not picked up). Typically, if Cascade delivered the data correctly, downstream issues are handled by those respective teams. However, Cascade's scope of monitoring does extend a bit into downstream to verify that what Cascade produced was consumed. For example, if SFMC regularly writes a "success" or "failure" log after processing a file, Cascade might check that. In the SFMC case, the team noticed failures in SFMC because of empty files flagging errors; they coordinated with the SFMC team and adjusted expectations on empty files. If a segment file fails to import into P2's Dynamo, Cascade would likely get an error callback and would retry or notify the P2 team.
- Consent/DSR Conflicts: A potential failure mode specific to privacy is if a customer's data is not correctly removed. For instance, if the Cascade ID linking fails (missing join), some data might not delete. Cascade's DSR process is robust (join across profiles ensures all linked data is found). If any dataset was missed due to a new table not in the script, that's a process gap. The team periodically audits that all new data sources are hooked into the DSR process. This isn't a runtime "failure" that halts a job, but a logical failure that would be caught in privacy reviews. So far, no incident, but the team remains vigilant. In terms of recovery, if they discovered later a table was not covered, they would run a corrective deletion on that table for past requests.
- Data Quality Issues: If a source data changes format or contains bad values (e.g., a column added/dropped), Cascade jobs could either fail or produce incorrect results. For quality issues not causing outright failure, Cascade relies on validation checks and manual review. The team often performs manual checks on key metrics each day (like record counts compared to usual, or segment sizes). If something looks off (e.g., suddenly zero records ingested from a busy source), that triggers investigation. They strive to catch these early to then either fix parsing logic or communicate with source owners.

Retries & Redundancy: Many of Cascade's scheduled jobs likely have a simple retry once strategy for transient errors. But more importantly, Cascade keeps data so that if a job fails today, running it tomorrow can often catch up (since the source usually can resend yesterday's data or keep it accessible). This provides a form of data redundancy. Also, multiple days of data are stored, so one day's failure doesn't wipe out history. They also occasionally run jobs on overlapping data as a safety net – e.g., a job might always read the last 2 days of data to ensure if it missed yesterday it will still ingest it. The specifics vary per pipeline.

Where feasible, the team has built idempotent processing: meaning re-running a job for the same date will not duplicate data (Delta merges or dedup logic handle it). This allows safe manual retries.

Monitoring for Failures: (More in Ops section) – Cascade uses dashboards (Splunk logs, daily reports) to catch failures. For example, a daily monitoring dashboard shows the status of each daily job (success/fail, record count) – a quick glance each morning by the on-call engineer reveals if anything needs attention. Additionally, automated alerts are set: e.g., if a Databricks job fails or a critical log error appears, an email/Teams alert goes to the Cascade ops group (like "P2 Retargeting ITT" distribution list).

SLAs, Schedules, and Critical Timing

The Cascade team works with certain timing assumptions:

- Daily Data Availability SLA: Have previous day's data fully processed by early morning (e.g., by 8 AM UTC). This ensures that if marketing wants to launch or analyze something each morning, the data is ready. It's not explicitly written, but implied by daily cadence.
- Campaign Response Time SLA: When Engagement Studio calls Cascade for a segment, Cascade should respond within a reasonable time (likely measured in seconds or minutes, not hours). If an audience is huge or complex, the API might take longer, but ideally they set expectation that campaign launch isn't held up too long. We can infer they aim for real-time or near-real-time segmentation for the control plane.
- DSR Compliance SLA: 24 hours (as mandated by policy) to remove a user's data after request. Cascade meets this by daily DSR runs; effectively ~<24h latency (often much less).
- Welcome Email SLA: PSUV pipeline ensures new user welcome outreach occurs within minutes (the doc says within minutes after registration). So they treat ~15-30 minutes as the SLA for that event-triggered scenario.
- Zero downtime for campaigns: A crucial non-functional SLA: Cascade must not have downtime that prevents campaigns from going out. This means even during deployments or maintenance, the system should be available. As a result, they avoid taking the system offline; any heavy backfills or changes are done in parallel or during safe windows to not block campaign operations.

Critical Path & Dependencies: As summarized, the critical path is: source data -> Cascade ingested -> segment generated -> delivered to channel. Dependencies:

- If any source ingestion fails, segments that depend on that source may be incomplete. The team coordinates closely in such cases. For example, if the Support Org data fails to import, and there's a campaign that evening relying on warranty info, they might either quickly fix it or, if not possible, let marketing know to exclude that criterion or delay the campaign.
- Consent data and profile linking are prerequisites for any segmentation – those run continuously, and any issue there (e.g., Cascade ID generation glitch) could affect everything. Thankfully, those are stable processes.

In practice, Cascade's design of independent pipelines and unified data store means most pipelines do not block others. Each adds incremental data. Engagement Studio campaigns will operate on whatever data is available. Therefore, a single pipeline's failure might degrade some campaigns but not bring the whole system to a halt. The system is somewhat graceful in degradation: e.g., if one data source is missing, campaigns using it might under-target users (since no one qualifies on that criterion), but other campaigns proceed normally. This reduces the likelihood of a total system outage; issues tend to be isolated.

Example Recovery Scenario: A real example from the knowledge base – the SFMC SFTP pipeline: Cascade was dropping files for SFMC, but none of the files were appearing in SFMC on certain days. Investigation showed the upstream Amazon Data Pipeline (responsible for moving those files) had stopped. The fix involved manually retriggering that pipeline and coordinating with the Instant Ink team (who provided data for those files) to ensure everything was back on schedule. Meanwhile, the Cascade team also started monitoring the SFTP directory directly so that if files ever stop appearing, they catch it quickly. This illustrates how they recover by working across team boundaries and then implement monitoring to mitigate recurrence.

Another scenario – Assume Role access: Suppose Cascade was supposed to read from another team's S3 but the IAM role wasn't correctly set (maybe the trust policy missing). The job fails with access denied. The team's procedure: check the IAM role config (Terraform file), update trust relationships or resource ARNs as needed, deploy it, and re-run. This usually fixes cross-account issues.

Overall, Cascade's core pipelines are robust and have run for years, so the team has a good playbook for each type of glitch. Combined with vigilant monitoring and a culture of daily checks and quick backfills, they maintain the flow of data so that campaign operations are uninterrupted.

4. Data Landscape

Primary Data Sources & Producers

Cascade integrates data from a wide array of HP's systems (and a few external sources). The "Data Patterns" listed in the architecture (Section 3) essentially enumerate the main sources. To recap, the major source data feeding Cascade includes:

- Support Organization Data: Warranty and Care Pack subscription information from HP's support systems (hosted in Azure). This data tells Cascade which customers have active warranties or support contracts. It's crucial for determining eligibility (e.g., marketing shouldn't sell extended warranty to someone who already has one, or should push it to someone whose warranty is ending). Producer: HP Support backend (data exported via Azure and MuleSoft to Cascade).
- HP Online Store Transactions: E-commerce sales data (purchases of printers, ink, etc. on HP's website) aggregated in the Enterprise Customer Data Platform (ECDP). This provides Cascade with up-to-date info on who bought what and when, a key input for cross-sell or upsell campaigns and revenue analytics. Producer: Adobe/ECDP pipeline that collects global store sales.
- WPP (Web Printing Platform) Data: Printer onboarding and registration events from HP's WPP system. This includes data like printer device IDs tied to customer HP accounts when they set up a printer. It essentially produces profile info linking customers to devices. Producer: WPP databases (likely an internal MySQL/SQLServer data source) that Cascade queries.
- Mastiff Campaign Data: Mastiff is an internal system (likely in HP's support or marketing org) that runs ML-based targeting. It sends daily output files with results of its targeting or campaigns (the doc calls it "Adobe Stores" but description implies an internal system named Mastiff). Producer: Mastiff pipelines via Data Hub (HP's internal SFTP data exchange).
- Branch.io Data: Third-party data for mobile app engagement (deep links). Branch provides metrics on how users interact with HP app links. Producer: Branch's API, delivering events data to Cascade on request.
- P2 In-App Engagement Data: HP's "P2" backend (part of the HP mobile app ecosystem) generates data on in-app messages delivered to users and their interactions. Producer: The P2 system dropping logs to S3 (likely from HP's cloud).
- Messaging Hub Data: Similar to P2, the Messaging Hub (possibly part of HP's unified messaging service) logs push notification events. Producer: Message Hub service.
- Telemetry Events (PSUV): Telemetry from devices (like PC registration events) captured by HP's data pipeline (firehose). Producer: HP's device telemetry systems (e.g., ISAAC for PC registration, or events from printers sending logs).
- Consent & Preference Data: Not explicitly listed in the eight patterns but extremely important: this comes from HP's privacy systems (could be a central consent database or various app-specific logs). Cascade likely gets daily feeds of consent changes for each identifier type (printers, PCs, apps, email, etc.). Producer: HP Consent management systems (could be a combination of databases and nightly exports).
- Customer Profile Data: Basic customer information (like HPID, email, country) – these might come from HP's account systems. Cascade likely ingests or accesses the HPID profile database (or gets snapshots) so it has the master list of customers. Producer: HP Account/HPID system (or ECDP which might also store profile info).
- DSR Requests: Feeds of IDs requesting deletion come from HP's privacy office or centralized system. Producer: Privacy request system (perhaps an internal portal or GDPR tool).
- Other Miscellaneous: There might be more minor sources like marketing subscription lists, external partner data (if HP gets data from partners like retail registrations), etc., but those are not detailed in the main docs. The eight patterns cover most major data flows.

All these sources feed raw data into Cascade. Each source has an owner team (Support team owns support data, etc.). Data contracts with these sources are mostly informal but important: e.g., Support team agrees to deliver daily files by X time in format Y; store data via ECDP has an agreed schema for transaction records. Cascade codifies these expected schemas in its ingestion code. When sources need to change (say add a column), they usually coordinate with Cascade team so the pipeline can be updated in tandem (this is part of "change sensitivity" further below).

Importantly, Cascade does not originate data on its own – it's a consumer and aggregator. The quality and completeness of Cascade's outputs depend on these inputs being provided reliably.
